Radiation Damage in MX
Macromolecular crystallography · teaching module

Radiation damage, from photon to lattice

Every absorbed X-ray photon deposits ~keV into a crystal that is held together by interactions worth a few kJ/mol. The damage proceeds in two overlapping regimes: specific (site-selective chemistry, visible in difference maps at low dose) and global (loss of diffracting power, visible in the data statistics). Dose, in grays (1 Gy = 1 J kg−1), is the metric against which both are tracked.1

Absorbed dose at 100 K unless noted. Log axis. Specific damage begins orders of magnitude before the global limits.

01Primary interactions

At 12.4 keV in protein crystals only a small fraction of interacting photons scatter elastically; most are absorbed photoelectrically. Energy is deposited by the electrons that result, not by the photon itself.

02Electron cascade and radiolysis

The crystal is ~30–70 % solvent, so most energy is deposited in water. Ionisation and excitation of water produce the radicals that attack the protein (indirect damage); direct ionisation of the protein produces holes and electrons in situ. At 100 K most damage is attributed to direct ionisation of residues and their associated water, since OH• cannot diffuse.17

Energetics

  • Photoelectron energy ≈ Eγ − EB; K-edges: C 0.28, N 0.41, O 0.54, S 2.47 keV. A 12.4 keV photon on O yields an ≈ 11.9 keV photoelectron.
  • Auger electrons from C/N/O: ≈ 0.25–0.5 keV, ranges of a few nm.
  • Each photoelectron can cause up to ~500 further ionisations (≈ 11.9 keV / 500 ≈ 24 eV each).1
  • Photoelectron range is a few µm: around a 1 µm beam, damage fell to zero at ~4 µm from the beam centre, and the dose-normalised damage rate fell 3× from a 15.6 µm to a 0.84 µm beam.16 Microbeams and microcrystals can therefore lose part of the dose by escape.15
  • Low-energy (3–20 eV) secondary electrons, below the ionisation threshold, still break bonds via resonant electron attachment (shown for DNA strand breaks).18

03Radical mobility: 100 K versus room temperature

Cryocooling does not prevent radical formation. It stops diffusion of OH• and other heavy radicals, but electrons (and, more slowly, holes) still migrate by tunnelling and hopping to electron-affinic sites. This is why metal centres and disulfides are reduced at 100 K.1,8

e⁻ OH• H• hole (h⁺) metal centre S–S Schematic. Rates are illustrative, not simulated.

Live tally

100 K

  • OH• trapped in amorphous solvent at 100 K.22
  • Electrons migrate to metal centres and disulfides; holes to electron-rich sites such as carboxylates.
  • Dose-rate dependence is weak: none detected by Sliz et al.;20 ~10 % shorter lifetime for a 10× higher dose rate in ferritin.31
  • H₂ gas formed in the sample is proposed as the main cause of high-resolution loss. At 50 K versus 100 K: disulfide damage reduced 4×, and 2.6× (elastase) and 3.9× (cubic insulin) higher dose tolerated by Rfree.14

Room temperature

  • OH•, H• and e⁻aq diffuse through solvent channels and react with surface residues.
  • Radiation sensitivity rises sharply above a transition near 200 K, consistent with solvent-coupled diffusion.23
  • Crystal lifetime ~26–113× shorter than at 100 K (lysozyme, 6–10 Gy s⁻¹).21
  • Dose-rate effects appear: lifetime increased with dose rate (60 % higher rate, 4× longer lifetime);21 short exposures can outrun part of the damage (up to half at 260 K with ~1 s collection).22,23

04Specific damage

Site-selective changes seen as negative Fo(Dn) − Fo(D1) difference density. Typical order at 100 K: metal reduction first; then disulfides elongate and break, Glu/Asp decarboxylate, Tyr loses its hydroxyl, and Met C–S bonds are cleaved.1,6,7,8 Within each class, susceptibility varies between sites;6 for acidic residues, active-site and crystal-contact sites are most sensitive and there is no correlation with solvent accessibility.10

05Global damage

Accumulated specific damage and lattice disorder reduce diffracting power, highest resolution first. Model: mean intensity at resolution d decays as ⟨I⟩ = ⟨I⟩₀ exp(−ln2 · D / (H d)), with H = 10 MGy Å⁻¹, so D½ ≈ 10d MGy.4,5,11

Observables

  • Intensity loss, starting at high resolution; I/σ(I) in the outer shell falls first.
  • Wilson B rises roughly linearly with dose; rate varies between crystals.3
  • Unit-cell volume expands, typically by a few tenths of a percent to ~1 %; rate is not transferable between crystals, so not a reliable dose metric.24,25 APPROX
  • Mosaicity often increases; not universal.
  • Scaling B (per-batch relative B in AIMLESS/XSCALE) becomes increasingly negative with frame number.
  • Rd rises with frame separation (Diederichs).26
  • Rising Rmerge, falling CC½ in the outer shell.

06Practical consequences

  • Plan dose, not exposure time. Calculate with RADDOSE-3D for the actual beam profile, crystal shape and composition.27
  • Spread dose. Helical (vector) scans translate the crystal during rotation so each image exposes fresh volume. Compared with standard rotation at SOLEIL PROXIMA 2A, helical scans extended usable resolution over the collection and preserved the anomalous signal for phasing; C–Br bond loss served as a sensitive damage monitor.32 With microbeams and translations the dose distribution is inhomogeneous; diffraction-weighted dose (RADDOSE-3D) models the decay, and offsetting the rotation axis from the beam by 1.25 × beam FWHM extended crystal lifetime.33 Also: multiple positions, multi-crystal merging, serial crystallography.
  • Collect redox-sensitive sites first, or composite structures from low-dose wedges of many crystals; verify with online spectroscopy.12,13
  • Microbeams on microcrystals can gain from photoelectron escape, larger at higher photon energy.15,16
  • Scavengers: mixed results at 100 K; styrene ineffective, tentative evidence for ascorbate in lysozyme.24 CASE-DEPENDENT
  • XFEL femtosecond pulses outrun global damage ("diffraction before destruction"),29 but metal sites can still be affected within longer pulses.30

Diagnosing specific damage in your data

  • Process the first and last wedges separately; inspect Fo(late) − Fo(early) at disulfides, metals, Glu/Asp, Met.
  • Look for negative peaks on Sγ, carboxyl O, metal ligands; positive peaks at alternative S positions.
  • Check metal–ligand distances against the expected oxidation state.
  • Track scaling B and Rd versus frame; truncate where the outer-shell CC½ or I/σ(I) degrades.
