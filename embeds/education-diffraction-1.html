From one molecule to a crystal: how diffraction builds up

Every image below is calculated from atomic coordinates of a model molecule in a 2D projection. Follow the five steps, or play the full sequence.

Model and what is left out

2D calculation, plane group p1 (equivalently, the zero-layer section of reciprocal space, which by the projection–slice theorem is the transform of the structure projected along the beam-normal axis): one molecule of 40 point atoms per rectangular cell, a = 40 Å, b = 30 Å. Atoms are Gaussian electron clouds (b = 15 Å², equal weight) with an adjustable isotropic B factor, so the amplitude carries exp(−(15 Å² + B)s²) with s = |q|/2 = sin θ/λ. The molecular transform is a direct sum over atoms; the lattice factor is evaluated analytically and averaged over each pixel, so unresolved fringes appear as their mean, as on a detector. Reciprocal-space units: q = 1/d (Å⁻¹, no 2π). Intensities are shown on a logarithmic scale; the region d > 90 Å is masked by a beamstop.

Not modelled: polarisation and absorption, mosaicity, beam divergence and bandwidth (spot width here comes only from crystal size), bulk solvent, symmetry other than p1, the third dimension, anomalous scattering, multiple scattering, radiation damage and counting noise.
