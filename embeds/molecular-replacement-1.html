Molecular replacement and the log-likelihood gain

How a search model is placed in an unknown crystal, and why the log-likelihood gain (LLG) decides whether the placement is real. Interactive figures compute the LLG live in your browser.

1The problem

A diffraction experiment measures intensities, I ∝ |F|2. The phases of the structure factors are lost. Molecular replacement (MR) borrows phases from a related, known structure: if the search model can be placed correctly in the unit cell of the unknown crystal, its calculated phases give a first electron-density map that refinement and model building then improve.

MR is the most common route to macromolecular phases (McCoy et al., 2017). Accurate predicted models have extended its reach to targets with no close homologue in the PDB (McCoy et al., 2022).

2Six parameters per copy

Placing a rigid model requires three rotation angles and three translation coordinates. A full six-dimensional search is expensive, so the problem is split (Rossmann & Blow, 1962; implemented with likelihood targets in Phaser, McCoy et al., 2007):

1. Rotation functionScore trial orientations. Intramolecular vectors do not depend on position, so orientation can be searched first.
2. Translation functionFor each candidate orientation, score positions in the cell. Symmetry copies now interfere correctly.
3. Packing checkReject placements that clash with symmetry mates.
4. Rigid-body refinementRefine orientation, position and model error (VRMS); rescore with the full LLG.

With several copies or components, the steps repeat, each search conditioned on the components already placed. The rotation signal is intrinsically weaker than the translation signal because the relative phases of symmetry-related copies are unknown at that stage (Read, 2001). A correct orientation can have a rotation-function Z-score (RFZ) below 4; it is confirmed by a clear translation solution.

3Why likelihood

Older targets (correlation coefficients, R factors) compare observed and calculated amplitudes as if the model were exact. Likelihood asks a different question: how probable are the observed data if the model is placed here, given that the model is incomplete and has coordinate errors?

The answer depends on one quantity, σA (Read, 1986): the correlation between the true and model structure factors as a function of resolution. In normalised form (E values, ⟨|E|2⟩ = 1), the true E lies in a complex Gaussian cloud centred on σAEC with variance 1 − σA2. The experiment measures only |E|, so what matters is the distribution of distances from the origin to points in that cloud.

4What the LLG measures

LLG = Σhkl ln [ p(Iobs | model placed here) / p(Iobs | same atoms placed randomly) ] The reference (null) hypothesis is a structure with the same atomic content but no information on positions (Wilson statistics). The intensity form, LLGI, uses intensities and their errors directly, including weak and negative measurements, without French-Wilson conversion (Read & McCoy, 2016).
  • One vote per reflection. The widget above gives one term of the sum. The LLG adds the votes of every reflection.
  • Interpretation. LLG is the natural log of a likelihood ratio: how much better the placed model explains the data than no model. Large and positive means the placement explains the data.
  • Wrong placements score negative on average. A wrong placement makes confident, wrong predictions; the data penalise it. The more accurate the model, the larger both the reward for the correct placement and the penalty for wrong ones.
  • Additive over components. LLG should increase as each correct copy or domain is added (Phaser documentation).
  • Why a threshold is needed. A search tests a very large number of trial placements. The best wrong placement is the maximum of many noisy scores, so a correct solution must stand clear of that noise. The more parameters searched, the higher the bar.

5The expected LLG: predicting success before searching

McCoy et al. (2017) derived the LLG expected from a correctly placed model. It depends only on f, Δ, the measurement errors and the number of reflections, so it can be calculated before any search.

eLLG = Σhkl ½ (Dobs σA)4 Approximation valid for the small DobsσA typical of difficult cases (Oeffner et al., 2018, eq. 3). Dobs ≤ 1 accounts for measurement error in each intensity; Dobs = 1 for perfect data.
The fourth power is the central point. Information per reflection scales as σA4. Halving σA cuts the contribution of that reflection 16-fold. Since σA4 ∝ f2, a model with half the scattering gives one quarter of the eLLG; two copies placed together give four times the eLLG of one.

Consequences

  • Model accuracy dominates. Δ enters through an exponential, raised to the fourth power.
  • Resolution has a ceiling. Reflections finer than about 1.8Δ add little; Phaser limits the search resolution accordingly.
  • Trimming can help. Removing wrong parts (flexible loops, low-confidence regions) costs some f but reduces the effective Δ, often raising eLLG.
  • Search order. Place the component with the highest eLLG first; later searches benefit from it.
  • Data quality counts. Weak, noisy intensities lower Dobs and therefore eLLG.
Checked numerically. What to try

  • Default (complete model, Δ 0.4 Å). Run the full search. Red appears only near the correct placement; the rest of the map is blue. The correct placement wins by a wide margin.
  • Raise Δ to 1.0 Å. The eLLG falls about tenfold. The correct peak shrinks to the height of noise peaks: the search can no longer tell.
  • Lower the fraction modelled to 0.3. Same effect through f2, even with an accurate model.
  • With Δ 1.0 Å, change dmin from 3.5 to 1.6 Å. The eLLG rises only from about 6 to 10: reflections beyond 1.8Δ carry almost no information. Watch the running-LLG panel flatten.
  • Watch the votes. At the correct placement, strong observed reflections line up with strong model reflections (green). At a wrong placement the points scatter and strong-model, weak-observed reflections vote heavily against it (red).

Thresholds in the toy differ from Phaser's because the toy has about 300 to 1,000 reflections and 3,600 trial placements in two parameters, against tens of thousands of reflections and millions of trials in six parameters for a real search. The behaviour is the same.

7Has it solved?

The translation-function Z-score (TFZ) is the number of standard deviations the LLGI of a placement lies above the mean of all trial placements. For the first component, TFZ ≈ √LLGI (approximate; Oeffner et al., 2018).

First component, non-polar space groups (six parameters searched). Oeffner et al. (2018), Table 1. Lower bars apply when fewer parameters are searched: about LLGI 50 / TFZ 7 in polar space groups and LLGI 30 / TFZ 5.5 in P1. Rule of thumb: LLGI of about ten times the number of parameters fixed. The Phaser documentation suggests TFZ 6 for the first component in monoclinic space groups.

  • Independence. LLG and TFZ only judge a model that has never been refined against the same data. Re-placing a refined model gives high scores that prove nothing.
  • Complications. Translational NCS, twinning and wrong assumptions about asymmetric-unit contents alter the statistics.
  • Confirmation. A placement is a starting point: check packing, map quality and Rfree behaviour during refinement.
