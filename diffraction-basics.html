Diffraction basics for biochemists

The physics behind our jargon: from waves and lattices to serial data and time-resolved difference maps Educational · Interactive

What we measure and what we want

We want the electron density of a macromolecule, ρ(r). We measure the intensities of X-rays scattered by many identical copies of that molecule arranged in a crystal. The density and the scattered wave are related by a Fourier transform. The detector records only intensities, so half the information (the phases) is lost and must be recovered by other means.

In one line: crystal → diffraction spots (amplitudes only) → add phases → Fourier transform → electron density map → atomic model.

Waves, phase and interference

X-rays are electromagnetic waves with wavelength λ around 1 Å (0.1 nm), comparable to interatomic distances. When waves scattered from different points overlap, their amplitudes add, taking their relative phase into account. In phase, they reinforce; half a wavelength out of phase, they cancel. The detector measures intensity, the squared magnitude of the summed amplitude.

Bragg's description treats the crystal as stacks of parallel planes with spacing d. Waves reflected from adjacent planes travel an extra path 2d sin θ. When that equals a whole number of wavelengths, all planes add constructively:

2d sin θ = nλ θ is half the scattering angle 2θ measured on the detector.

With many planes, constructive interference becomes extremely sharp: a crystal with N repeats concentrates the scattered intensity into peaks whose height scales as N2 and whose width scales as 1/N. This is why crystals give spots, and why small crystals give broader, weaker spots.

Bragg's law and the sharpening effect of many repeats

What scatters: electrons

  • X-rays scatter from electrons, so the experiment images electron density, not nuclei. Heavier atoms (S, metals) scatter more strongly; hydrogen has one electron and is barely visible except at very high resolution.
  • Each element has an atomic form factor f, equal to its electron count at zero angle and falling off at higher angle because the electron cloud has finite size.
  • Thermal motion and disorder smear atoms further. This is described by the B-factor (atomic displacement parameter), B = 8π2⟨u2⟩, where ⟨u2⟩ is the mean-square displacement. Higher B means weaker high-angle scattering.
  • Near an element's absorption edge, f gains extra real and imaginary components (f′, f″). This anomalous scattering is the basis of SAD and MAD phasing.

The crystal: lattice, unit cell, symmetry

  • Lattice: the array of points by which the contents repeat in 3D.
  • Unit cell: the repeating box, defined by edge lengths a, b, c and angles α, β, γ. Protein cells are typically tens to hundreds of Å on a side.
  • Asymmetric unit (ASU): the smallest part that generates the whole cell by applying the symmetry operators. It may contain one or several copies of the molecule.
  • Space group: the set of symmetry operations (rotations, screw axes, translations) relating copies within the cell. Proteins are chiral, so their crystals lack mirrors and inversion centres. Only the 65 Sohncke space groups are possible.
  • Solvent content: protein crystals are typically 30 to 70% solvent. This is why they are fragile, why ligands can soak in, and why molecules can still move inside them, which makes time-resolved experiments possible.
  • Miller indices (hkl): integers that label a family of lattice planes, and the corresponding diffraction spot (reflection).
  • Systematic absences: reflections forced to zero by screw axes or centring, used to help assign the space group.

Reciprocal space and resolution

Each reflection hkl corresponds to a point in reciprocal space, at distance 1/dhkl from the origin. A large unit cell in real space gives closely spaced reflections in reciprocal space, and vice versa. The diffraction pattern is a picture of reciprocal space.

  • Resolution is quoted as the smallest d (in Å) to which reflections are measured. Smaller is better: "2.0 Å data" contain more detail than "3.0 Å data". High resolution means high scattering angle, at the edge of the detector.
  • Rough guide for proteins: about 3.5 Å and worse, secondary structure and backbone trace; about 2.5 Å, side chains clear; about 1.5 Å and better, individual atoms resolved, some hydrogens visible near 1 Å. These are rules of thumb; map quality also depends on data quality and B-factors.
  • The Ewald sphere (radius 1/λ) determines which reciprocal lattice points satisfy the diffraction condition for a given crystal orientation. A still image records only the points cut by the sphere. See the Ewald sphere construction.
  • Each reciprocal lattice point is not a mathematical point: it is smeared by the crystal's finite size (the shape transform), mosaicity, and the beam's divergence and bandwidth. See the nanocrystal diffraction explorer.

Structure factors and the Fourier transform

The wave scattered into reflection hkl is the structure factor, a complex number with an amplitude |F| and a phase φ. It is the sum of contributions from every atom j in the unit cell at fractional position (xj, yj, zj):

F(hkl) = Σj fj exp[2πi(hxj + kyj + lzj)] = |F| eiφ

The electron density is recovered by the inverse Fourier transform, summing over all measured reflections:

ρ(x,y,z) = (1/V) Σhkl |F(hkl)| exp[iφ(hkl) − 2πi(hx + ky + lz)]
  • Every reflection contributes to every point in the map, and every atom contributes to every reflection. Missing or bad reflections degrade the whole map, not one region.
  • Stopping the sum at a resolution limit blurs the map and adds ripples (series termination).
  • Friedel's law: without anomalous scattering, |F(hkl)| = |F(−h−k−l)|. Anomalous differences between these Friedel pairs carry phase information.
  • The measured intensity is I(hkl) ∝ |F(hkl)|2, after corrections (polarisation, Lorentz, absorption, and partiality for stills).

1D Fourier synthesis: resolution and the importance of phases

A 1D "unit cell" (40 Å) containing five atoms. Structure factors are calculated to 1 Å, then summed back to a density using only reflections to the chosen resolution, with the chosen phases.

The phase problem

The detector records |F|2; the phases are lost. As the "amplitudes of A, phases of B" option above shows, the phases determine where density appears, so they cannot be ignored. Ways to obtain them:

  • Molecular replacement (MR): place a known similar structure (or a predicted model, such as from AlphaFold) in the unknown cell and use its calculated phases as a starting point. Most protein structures are now solved this way. See molecular replacement and the LLG.
  • Experimental phasing: SAD and MAD use anomalous scatterers (Se-Met, S, metals, halides); SIR and MIR use isomorphous heavy-atom derivatives.
  • Direct methods: standard for small molecules; for proteins only at atomic resolution.
Model bias: phases calculated from a model pull the map towards that model. A ligand placed in the model can appear in a map it was used to phase. Omit maps and Rfree are the main safeguards.

Resolution at the detector

Assumes the beam hits the detector centre and the detector is perpendicular to the beam. Defaults: EIGER2 XE 16M active area (4148 × 4362 pixels of 75 µm). d = λ / (2 sin θ), with 2θ = arctan(r/D).

Dose and radiation damage

  • Dose is absorbed energy per unit mass, in gray (Gy = J/kg). Typical values are kGy to MGy.
  • At 100 K, the commonly used limits are about 20 MGy (Henderson) and about 30 MGy (Garman) for halving the diffracted intensity. Room-temperature crystals tolerate far less dose, because radicals diffuse.
  • Specific damage (disulfide breakage, decarboxylation, metal reduction) can occur well before the diffraction fades, and can be mistaken for biology.
  • XFELs outrun most damage by finishing the measurement within the pulse; serial synchrotron methods spread the dose over many crystals.

See radiation damage in macromolecular crystallography and diffraction spots fading with dose.

Serial crystallography

Rotation crystallography collects a complete dataset from one crystal (or a few), rotating it through the beam. Serial crystallography collects one or a few still images from each of thousands of crystals in random orientations, then merges them. This allows room-temperature data, micro- and nanocrystals, low dose per crystal, and time-resolved experiments where each crystal is used once.

Models, maps and refinement

  • Refinement adjusts the atomic model (positions, B-factors, occupancies) so that calculated amplitudes |Fc| match observed |Fo|, subject to stereochemical restraints.
  • R-factor (Rwork): disagreement between |Fo| and |Fc|. Rfree is the same statistic for a set of reflections (about 5%) never used in refinement; it detects overfitting.
  • Occupancy: fraction of unit cells in which an atom or conformation is present. Alternate conformations, partially bound ligands, and intermediates in time-resolved data are modelled with occupancies below 1.
  • 2mFo−DFc map: best estimate of the density, used to build the model.
  • mFo−DFc difference map: positive peaks show density missing from the model; negative peaks show model with no supporting density.
  • Omit map: calculated with part of the model (often a ligand) removed, to reduce model bias.
  • Map contour levels are quoted in σ (rmsd of the map), for example 1.0σ for 2mFo−DFc and ±3.0σ for difference maps.

Time-resolved crystallography

  • Pump-probe: a trigger (usually a laser pulse acting on a light-sensitive protein or caged compound) starts the reaction; an X-ray probe follows after a controlled time delay.
  • Mix-and-inject / mix-and-probe: crystals are mixed with substrate or ligand and probed after a delay set by flow rates or travel distance. Diffusion into the crystal limits the fastest time point, so smaller crystals give better time resolution. See mixing device timing simulations.
  • Dark / reference dataset: the same crystals without triggering, collected under identical conditions.
  • Isomorphous difference map: Fo(t) − Fo(dark), with phases from the dark model. It shows what changed at time t and is more sensitive than comparing two separately refined models. It requires the crystals to remain isomorphous (same cell and packing).
  • Activated fraction α: only part of the molecules react. Extrapolated structure factors, Fextr = Fdark + (Flight − Fdark)/α, estimate the pure intermediate for model building.
  • A time series of difference maps can be analysed for kinetic intermediates and rates ("molecular movies").
